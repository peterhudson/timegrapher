# When to see a watchmaker

Four verdicts. Give one, plainly, at the top of the answer, then the
reasons. The thresholds assume a **sound measurement** (checked with
`doctor`, the right lift angle and beat rate), a **fully wound** watch that
has **settled**, and a modern 28,800 vph calibre unless said otherwise.
For older 18,000–21,600 vph watches, allow somewhat lower amplitude and
higher beat error (see [reading-the-numbers.md](reading-the-numbers.md)).

The thresholds are rules of thumb, not a contract. Weigh them together: one
borderline figure on an otherwise healthy watch is "keep an eye on it";
several together, or a trend, moves it up a step.

## Fine

The watch is healthy. Wear it and enjoy it.

- Amplitude dial up 250° or more (fully wound); vertical within about 50°
  of dial up.
- Beat error under about 0.7 ms (1.0 ms on an older watch).
- Rate averaged over positions within what the owner is happy with, and
  the positions within about 15–20 s/d of each other (more on a budget or
  clone calibre).
- A straight, steady trace across the whole run.
- No periodic pattern beyond a few degrees.

What to say: "It's in good order. The amplitude is healthy, which is the
main thing; the rest is regulation and it's doing fine."

## Keep an eye on it

Nothing to do now, but worth a re-test in six to twelve months or if its
timekeeping changes.

- Amplitude dial up 220–250° fully wound, steady.
- Beat error 0.7–1.5 ms.
- Positional spread 20–30 s/d on a watch that should do better.
- Amplitude drop from full wind to 24 h of 40–60°.
- A small regular wave or once-a-minute dip that isn't affecting
  timekeeping.
- Last serviced over about five years ago but measuring well.

What to say: "It's running fine, but the amplitude is on the low side of
healthy. That's usually the first sign the oil is ageing. Test it again in
six months; if it's dropped further, book a service."

## Service soon

Book it in within the next few months. It's fine to keep wearing it
meanwhile, unless it gets worse.

- Amplitude dial up under about 220° fully wound, or vertical under about
  180–200°.
- Amplitude falling between tests months apart.
- A large difference between dial up and dial down (more than about
  20°), or between horizontal and vertical (more than about 60–70°).
- Amplitude dropping more than about 60° from full wind to 24 h, or a
  noticeably short power reserve.
- Positional spread over about 30 s/d on a good calibre, or rate outside
  what the owner can live with: regulation, usually done with a service.
- Beat error over about 1.5–2 ms.
- A clear periodic fault (a reliable once-a-minute or once-an-hour
  pattern of more than a few degrees or s/d) confirmed on a long run.
- Still gaining hundreds of seconds a day after demagnetising.
- No service in the last 7–10 years, whatever the numbers say: oil doesn't
  last forever, and wear on dry pivots is what makes the bill bigger (check:
  makers' intervals range from about 5 to 10 years).

What to say: "It needs a service. The amplitude is low, which means it's
working harder than it should to keep going, and that's how wear starts.
It'll keep running for now, but the sooner it's done the less it'll
cost."

If the watch is under warranty (Rolex gives five years, Omega up to five
or more (check current terms)), send it back to the maker rather than an
independent; say what was measured.

## Stop wearing it

Stop wearing it and take it in. Carrying on can turn a service into a
repair.

- It stops, or stops in some positions.
- Amplitude very low: under about 150–160° dial up fully wound.
- Signs of knocking: amplitude over about 330° with a jumping or doubled
  trace, or a double tick you can hear.
- A sudden change after a knock or drop: amplitude much lower in some
  positions, a big jump in rate, a beat error that wasn't there before.
- Very erratic: a scattered, wandering trace with low amplitude, not
  fixed by re-clamping, re-winding or demagnetising.
- Grinding, scraping or rattling noises, a rotor you can hear rubbing, a
  crown that won't wind or feels gritty.
- Water or condensation under the crystal (not a timegrapher finding, but
  the most urgent of all: get it to a watchmaker the same day).

What to say: "Stop wearing it and take it to a watchmaker. Something is
catching or has been damaged, and running it like this can wear or break
parts that are expensive to replace."

## What not to do

- Don't send a watch to be serviced on the strength of one photo of a
  cheap timegrapher. Test properly first.
- Don't let a high rate in one position worry anyone. Rate is regulation;
  amplitude is health.
- Don't open the case back. Water resistance, dust and a slipped
  screwdriver across a balance cock are all expensive.
