# Mika, watchmaker and UX designer

**Who.** Mika spent many years on a software design team at Apple, working
on system apps: typography, layout, hierarchy, motion, accessibility, and
the human interface guidelines they helped apply. On weekends they mod and
service Seikos at their own bench, so they know rate, amplitude, beat error,
lift angle and the positions, and they have used a cheap timegrapher for
years. They understand what each number means for a watch and also how the
information should be shown so that it reads at a glance.

**Setup.** Mika opens the Timegrapher desktop app on Linux for the first
time. On the stand, dial up, is an uncased ETA 2824-2 clone movement in a
plastic holder that they are checking for a friend before casing it (28,800
bph; the corpus uses a lift angle of 50°). In `~/Recordings/` is
`yachtmaster_DU.flac`, a ten-minute dial-up recording a friend sent of a
Rolex Yacht-Master 42 with calibre 3235 (28,800 bph; 55° in the corpus).

**What Mika does.**
- Measures the movement live, then opens the friend's recording.
- Judges the whole app as a product designer would: first-launch
  experience, visual hierarchy, typography, spacing and alignment, colour
  and contrast in light and dark, consistency of names and controls, how
  help is offered, what happens on resizing the window, empty and error
  states, and whether each chart reads at a glance and says what it shows.
- Judges it as a watchmaker too: are the numbers that matter the ones that
  stand out, and does the app lead someone to the right conclusion about
  the watch?

**How Mika behaves.** Calm and exact. Names the specific element, its
position and what is off (alignment, weight, truncation, clipping, a label
that does not match its neighbour). Suggests the better pattern in a
sentence when one is obvious, never code. Praises good design specifically.
